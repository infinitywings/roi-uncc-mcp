---
title: AI-Assisted Red Team Evaluation Framework
---

Modern electric grids are increasingly software-defined and interconnected, which means that subtle weaknesses in control logic or communications can translate rapidly into physical risk. Traditional security testing still relies heavily on scripted fault scenarios, leaving planners without a reliable way to gauge how their defences hold up against adaptive adversaries. The proposed evaluation framework closes this gap by connecting an AI-assisted attacker to a high-fidelity co-simulation environment so that the grid can be stressed with dynamically generated campaigns rather than predetermined playbooks.

The framework integrates machine-intelligence components with industry-grade simulators such as GridLAB-D, GridPACK, or ns-3 via standard co-simulation interfaces. This architecture keeps electrical, communication, and market layers synchronised while allowing the attacker to observe the same telemetry a defender would ordinarily expose—phasor measurements, feeder loads, switch states, and other operational data. Armed with that view, the AI planner reasons about timing, asset selection, and coordination, then issues attack primitives such as distributed energy resource setpoint changes, message injections, or network reconnaissance.

Three design pillars underpin the effort. First, the co-simulation remains modular: grid models, communication overlays, and controller configurations can be swapped in and out so that researchers can evaluate a distribution feeder one week and a wide-area protection scheme the next. Second, the attacker operates through a governed tool registry that advertises legitimate primitives and enforces safety constraints. This keeps the experimentation space realistic while still granting the planner latitude to invent new combinations. Third, the defensive posture is fully instrumented. Protection states, DER responses, communication delays, and disruption scores are captured continuously, enabling quantitative comparisons between defensive strategies.

Early experiments confirm the value of this dynamic approach. When the attacker is allowed to reason in real time, it uncovers timing windows and coordination sequences that pre-scripted tests miss entirely. At the same time, the grid’s physical reaction—voltage excursions, overload trips, islanding events—depends closely on controller logic and DER constraints, underscoring the need to ground AI behaviour in detailed physics rather than abstract models. By packaging reference scenarios alongside extensibility hooks, the framework ensures that utilities, researchers, and vendors can evaluate their innovations under attack pressure that evolves as quickly as the systems they are trying to protect.

The expected outcome is a reusable playbook for resilience assessment. With this framework, stakeholders can run repeatable, fully instrumented campaigns in which an AI adversary adapts to their defences, reveals residual weaknesses, and validates remediation steps before they are deployed on real infrastructure. This elevates grid security testing from static compliance exercises to a living, adversarial rehearsal that keeps pace with both technological change and adversarial ingenuity.
